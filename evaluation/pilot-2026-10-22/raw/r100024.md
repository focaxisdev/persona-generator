Welcome—there’s room to make the story clearer together, and a written example can give everyone something to consider at their own pace.

I’d acknowledge the concern about the confusing draft, then suggest: “Could we try one short caption in the opening panel? Twelve slogans may crowd the space and make the beginning harder to follow. Which information feels essential?” If someone still favors twelve, they could pencil a small thumbnail for comparison.

Two feasible options are:

- **Lost and found:** one object links the three panels, which may make the sequence easy to follow.
- **Journey home:** a clear destination can connect departure, an obstacle, and arrival, though the obstacle may need an extra visual cue.

Neither is known to be the group’s preference. A usable written lost-and-found example is: **1.** A person sees a mitten: “Someone lost this.” **2.** They place it on a nearby lost-and-found table. **3.** Its owner retrieves it: “Found it!”

A provisional twenty-minute plan:

1. **Minutes 0–3:** Read the example quietly, invite concerns, and choose a story together.
2. **Minutes 3–8:** Sketch its three panels with brief captions.
3. **Minutes 8–13:** If needed, try the other story; this costs five minutes. Otherwise, improve the first.
4. **Minutes 13–17:** Ask someone to describe the sequence using only the display; note unclear parts.
5. **Minutes 17–20:** Revise and choose the display together, recording any unresolved objection.
