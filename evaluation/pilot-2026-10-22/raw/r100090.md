Welcome—there’s time to make a small game together, and the confusing draft gives us something concrete to improve.

I’d suggest keeping the twelve slogans on scrap paper for now. A single small card may become difficult to read; could we reduce the rules to a goal, a turn, and an ending? The group can decide which wording helps. For the participant who finds fast explanations difficult, provide a written example and time to read it before inviting questions.

Two feasible three-card options:

- **Matching:** Draw circles on two cards and a triangle on one. Shuffle face down; the player reveals two cards. Matching circles wins; otherwise replace them and allow one final attempt.
- **Sorting:** Draw a circle, triangle, and square. Shuffle face up; the player arranges them according to a written target order. This offers visible information but needs an agreed challenge to remain interesting.

**Written example for matching:** “You reveal a circle and a triangle. They differ, so turn both face down. You have one attempt left.”

A provisional twenty-minute plan:

1. **0–3:** Hear concerns, choose an option, and agree on its goal. Neither option has an established preference.
2. **3–8:** Make three cards and write brief rules plus an example on scrap paper.
3. **8–13:** Read, play, and note confusing moments or disagreement.
4. **13–18:** Choose whether to revise or spend five minutes testing the alternative.
5. **18–20:** Select one playable draft and record unresolved concerns.
