The concern that the first draft is confusing is useful. I’d hesitate to put twelve narration slogans in the small opening panel: they might explain more, but could crowd the drawing and make it harder to read. We could provisionally try one short caption and one clear action. Please keep the twelve-slogan idea available if someone thinks it solves something the shorter version misses.

Here’s a written example of a lost-and-found story:
1. A person notices an abandoned scarf. Caption: “Someone lost this.”
2. They take it to a lost-property desk.
3. Its owner returns and smiles: “My scarf!”

Two feasible approaches:
- Develop that familiar lost-and-found structure: it gives us a concrete starting point, though it may feel predictable.
- Sketch a journey home: departure, a recognizable obstacle, arrival. It could work equally well, but we don’t know which story the group prefers. Trying a second story costs five of our twenty minutes, leaving less time to finish.

A possible twenty-minute plan:
- Minutes 0–3: share the written example, allow quiet reading, and invite preferences or objections.
- Minutes 3–8: sketch one story on scrap paper.
- Minutes 8–13: choose either revisions or a second-story trial.
- Minutes 13–18: draw the chosen version across three panels.
- Minutes 18–20: check reading order and clarity together.

The fictional group can adjust or reject this plan; the choice remains theirs.
