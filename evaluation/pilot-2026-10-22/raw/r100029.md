Welcome. Here is one provisional approach for the group to consider.

The confusing first draft deserves attention. Twelve narration slogans in a small opening panel may make it harder to follow, although we cannot tell without seeing the sketch. Consider keeping one short setup sentence and moving essential information into the drawings or later panels. The teammate may have useful ideas within those slogans; ask which information readers most need first.

A written example can give everyone time to follow without relying on a fast spoken explanation. For instance, a lost-and-found story could read:

1. **Opening:** Someone notices an abandoned scarf. Caption: “Whose scarf is this?”
2. **Middle:** They hold it up beside a simple “Found” sign.
3. **Ending:** Its owner returns and thanks them.

Both options are feasible. Lost-and-found offers a clear problem and resolution; journey-home can use recognizable landmarks to show progress, but may need clearer transitions. Neither is known to be the group's preference. A second sketch could reveal a better fit, but costs five minutes that could otherwise improve readability.

If the group agrees, use the twenty minutes this way:

- **Minutes 0–3:** Write the two options down, invite preferences and concerns, and choose a starting story together.
- **3–8:** Sketch three panels with brief written captions.
- **8–10:** Read silently, then identify confusing parts.
- **10–15:** Revise, or try the other story if the group judges that worthwhile.
- **15–20:** Choose and clarify one display, checking that its sequence makes sense without verbal explanation.

The group retains the final choice.
